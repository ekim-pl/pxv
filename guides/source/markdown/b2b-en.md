# Business fleet deployment

<!-- print:opener -->
## Decide what the fleet is meant to do

This guide is for company leaders, IT and security owners, procurement teams, and service partners deciding whether and how to manage a fleet of privacy-focused Android phones. It describes a decision and operating model; it is not a security certification, a device acceptance record, or a substitute for an organization's own risk review.

Start with work that employees need to complete, the data they handle, the places they work, and the services they must reach. Describe the intended users and support model before choosing a phone policy. A policy should express approved profiles, apps, settings, network requirements, recovery expectations, and checks in terms the organization can review.

Use a small, explicit first deployment. Identify the business owner, technical operator, privacy or security reviewer, help desk, and the person who accepts each device. Assign a named decision maker for exceptions. A vendor can prepare a device, but the employer remains responsible for deciding which data and work belong on it.

## Choose a deployment model

| Model | Suitable when | Decisions to make |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Company-owned and assigned | The employer buys and supports a defined set of devices | Eligibility, replacement, acceptable use, employee privacy, return and wipe authority |
| Personally owned with managed work use | Existing employee devices must support an approved work use | Supported models, separation of work and personal data, support boundaries, and what the employer can observe or remove |
| Shared or role-based devices | Shifts, field work, or a fixed task uses a phone | Named custodian, sign-in and handoff, cleaning, offline access, loss reporting, and account separation |

Do not assume that an Android user profile is a complete mobile-device-management boundary. Confirm the selected OS, policy, applications, and support tools provide the controls the organization needs. Record unsupported requirements as a decision to narrow scope, select another platform, or stop the rollout.

## Specify policy and responsibilities

Create a reviewed policy from the organization's actual requirements. Define each user profile by work purpose; list only apps that have an approved source, exact version and signer evidence, where available. Specify language, network routes, sensitive permissions, support contacts, maintenance windows, and manual checks. Avoid broad requirements such as "secure" or "compliant" without a measurable test and an accountable reviewer.

The starter policy in a branded fork is a structural template. It does not decide an employer's legal basis, retention period, employee notice, acceptable-use terms, regulatory scope, application trust, or recovery service. Review those decisions with the relevant internal owners before a device receives company data.

Agree a responsibility matrix before procurement:

- **Business owner:** approves the use case, rollout size, service expectations, and exceptions.
- **IT operator:** maintains the project, policy, device inventory, exact software inputs, and support process.
- **Security/privacy reviewers:** assess data flows, access, retention, employee information, and incident handling.
- **Supplier or integrator:** documents its tasks and evidence; it does not inherit the employer's decisions by default.
- **Device user:** accepts the device, sets personal credentials privately, reports issues, and follows the agreed return process.

Keep device-specific acceptance evidence separate from a generic brochure or product guide. For each phone, retain its observed identity, policy and software snapshot, unresolved checks, operator signoff, recipient acceptance, and custody outcome in the organization's controlled records.

## Pilot, then scale

Use a pilot with representative roles and supported devices. Begin with test accounts and synthetic information. Check the normal work tasks, profile separation, connectivity, updates, lost-device response, support access, battery and accessories, accessibility, language, onboarding time, and employee understanding. Exercise recovery with representative app data on a disposable test device before promising a recovery time or data coverage.

Set go/no-go criteria before the pilot. For example: all required checks have named evidence; support can identify the affected device and owner; the update and replacement process is rehearsed; the recovery method has been demonstrated for the data categories in scope; and remaining gaps have an accountable owner and accepted treatment. A successful install alone is not a rollout decision.

Scale in controlled waves. Keep separate cohorts for materially different policies or operating environments. Recheck the approved policy and artifact versions at each wave, review exceptions and failures, and use fresh device-specific plans and acceptance. A fleet list is not permission to apply a change to every phone at once.

## Operate the fleet and handle change

Assign an owner to updates, app approvals, support hours, loss and theft reports, replacement, employee departure, and policy changes. Set review intervals based on the organization's risk and update commitments. Changes to OS or app versions, profile purpose, permissions, VPN, recovery methods, or vendor access may require a new review and evidence.

Use the platform's supported backup path and test each data category that the organization promises to recover. Backup status does not prove every app's data can be restored. App exclusions, hardware-bound keys, account recovery, passkeys, MFA, eSIMs, and service-side data may need separate treatment. Keep personal recovery material under the user's or organization's approved custody model, and restrict backup access to named roles.

Plan offboarding before rollout. Identify who can revoke work accounts or tokens, remove work information where supported, recover or transfer company-owned records, return accessories, update inventory, and retire the device. Personal content must be handled under the disclosed agreement and applicable organizational process. A project record or OS reinstall is not a general phone-data erasure or recovery guarantee.

## Procurement and service questions

Ask providers to state the exact supported device and OS scope, update policy, software and signing provenance, data paths, administrator access, support response targets, backup and restore limits, subcontractors, incident notification path, service end date, and export or exit process. Ask for evidence that matches the configuration being purchased. Marketing language and a sample report do not establish acceptance of your devices.

Compare total operating cost across hardware, enrollment, training, user support, accessories, connectivity, replacements, software review, recovery storage, audit evidence, and end-of-life handling. Include time for employees to set credentials privately and for operators to complete physical checks. Trial the real procurement and support workflow before committing the full fleet.

## Recommended decision record

For each deployment wave, record the business purpose, covered roles, device and OS scope, policy owner, approved applications, data categories, identity and access model, update and support plan, recovery objective and tested coverage, privacy notice, exception process, cost owner, acceptance evidence location, and retirement criteria. Mark each item as approved, pending, or out of scope with a named decision owner.

If a requirement cannot be measured, assigned, supported, and recovered within the agreed boundary, reduce the deployment scope or choose a different solution. Keep generic product material separate from the evidence that authorizes a specific device for work.
